Currently, the KL expansion is computed prior to any work with the FE space. A grid on the domain of the FE space is discretized at regularly spaced points and the discrete covariance eigenvalue problem is solved. Then in NGSolve those values are overlaid on the fine FE space and interpolated.

## KL expansion + NGSolve workflow

- `cartesian_grid_2d(nx, ny)` creates the KL grid independently of the finite-element mesh. In the example, `nx = ny = 16`, and `np.linspace(0, 1, 16)` supplies equally spaced $x$ and $y$ coordinates, including the boundary.
- `np.meshgrid` forms all $(x_i,y_i)$ pairs. These 256 physical locations are the points where the random field is represented.
- The KL grid points do **not** need to be NGSolve vertices. They are a fixed background grid used to define the coefficient field; the NGSolve mesh is created separately.
- The grid choice does matter numerically: its bounds must cover the PDE domain, and its spacing must be fine enough to resolve the covariance length scale and the field variation. A very coarse grid makes the random field artificially coarse.
- At the KL points, form the covariance matrix
  $C_{ij}=\sigma^2\exp(-\lVert x_i-x_j\rVert/\ell)$, where $\ell$ is the correlation length.
- Solve the discrete eigenproblem $Cq_j=\lambda_jq_j$ and retain the largest eigenpairs. This is a point-grid KL approximation, not an FE-based KL eigenproblem, so this version does not use an FE mass matrix.
- Draw independent $\xi_j\sim N(0,1)$ and sample
  $Z=\mu+\sum_j\sqrt{\lambda_j}\,\xi_jq_j$ at the KL grid points.
- Set $\kappa=\exp(Z)$ so the diffusion coefficient remains positive.
- `VoxelCoefficient` wraps the $(ny,nx)$ array and linearly interpolates it using physical $(x,y)$ coordinates. NGSolve therefore evaluates $\kappa$ wherever its quadrature points occur, even though those points and mesh nodes differ from the KL grid.
- Use `kappa` in the Poisson weak form:
  $\int_\Omega\kappa\,\nabla u\cdot\nabla v\,dx=\int_\Omega fv\,dx$.

### What happens on the two finite-element levels

- First create a coarse triangular NGSolve mesh. Uniformly refine that mesh once to obtain the nested fine mesh, then create an $H^1$ finite-element space on each mesh.
- `kappa` is technically an NGSolve `CoefficientFunction` (`VoxelCoefficient`). Its values came from the discrete KL eigenvalues/eigenvectors, and `VoxelCoefficient` interpolates those values in physical space.
- Reuse this one `kappa` object when building both bilinear forms. During quadrature, NGSolve evaluates the interpolated coefficient at the coarse quadrature points to assemble $A_c$, and at the fine quadrature points to assemble $A_f$:
  $(A_\ell)_{ij}=\int_\Omega\kappa(x)\,\nabla\phi_j^{(\ell)}\cdot\nabla\phi_i^{(\ell)}\,dx$, $\ell\in\{c,f\}$.
- Start with the fine-grid equation $A_fx_f=b_f$. A Gauss--Seidel sweep approximately solves this equation by updating
  $x_f\leftarrow x_f+S_f(b_f-A_fx_f)$, where $S_f$ is the Gauss--Seidel smoother.
- Compute the fine residual $r_f=b_f-A_fx_f$.
- Restrict the residual with $r_c=P^Tr_f$. Here $P$ is the FE prolongation matrix from the nested coarse $H^1$ space to the fine $H^1$ space.
- Solve the coarse error equation $A_ce_c=r_c$.
- Prolong the coarse correction back to the fine FE space, $e_f=Pe_c$, and update $x_f\leftarrow x_f+e_f$.
- Apply post-smoothing on the fine grid to reduce fine-scale error introduced or left behind by the coarse correction.
- The operations $P^T$ and $P$ act on the **PDE residual and solution correction only**. They do not project, restrict, modify, or resample the KL eigenvectors, the random variables $\xi_j$, or `kappa`.
- Therefore, the projection has no direct effect on the KL expansion. Its effect is only indirect: the already-fixed coefficient $\kappa(x,\omega)$ is contained in both $A_c$ and $A_f$, so it influences every smoothing and coarse-correction calculation.
